    Lye & water

    Sodium lactate (60% liquid): 1–3% of oil weight makes a harder bar that unmolds sooner. Don't go over 3%; too much can make bars brittle or crumbly. If you use the dry powder, use half as much.

    Citric acid (1–3%) cuts soap scum in hard water. It uses up some lye, so the calculator adds extra to make up for it.

    Common starting points: 5% superfat, 33% lye concentration (about 2 : 1 water to lye).

      Before you mix

      • Weigh everything on a scale, never by volume.
      • Always add lye to water, never water to lye. Wear goggles and gloves.
      • Saponification values are typical averages. For a new recipe, cross-check the lye amount in a second calculator.

      Temperatures

      Bring the lye water and the oils to about the same temperature, within 10°F (5°C) of each other, before you combine them.

      SituationLye water & oilsWhy
      Standard recipe90–110°F (32–43°C)Easy to control, traces at a moderate pace.
      Swirls or detailed designs85–95°F (29–35°C)Cooler batter stays fluid longer.
      Milk, honey, sugar or beer added80–90°F (27–32°C)Sugars heat up and can scorch or overheat.
      High in butters, beeswax or stearic acid100–120°F (38–49°C)Hard fats can firm up and look like trace too early.
      Adding sodium lactateUnder 130°F (54°C)Stir it into the lye water once it has cooled to this.

      Gel phase: for a gelled bar (brighter colour, slightly translucent), cover the mold and wrap it in a towel. To avoid gel (matte, creamier look), put the mold in the fridge or a cool room for the first 24 hours.

      How to make it

      1. Set up safely. Put on goggles, gloves and long sleeves, open a window, and keep kids and pets out. Use stainless steel, glass or #5 PP plastic, never aluminium.
      2. Weigh everything first. , of , of oils and fats.
      3. Dissolve extras in the water. Before adding the lye, stir into the water until dissolved.
      4. Make the lye solution. Slowly add the lye to the water, never water to lye, and stir until clear. It will fume and get very hot (up to about 200°F / 93°C). Don't breathe the fumes; leave it to cool somewhere safe.
      5. Add sodium lactate. Once the lye water is under 130°F (54°C), stir in of sodium lactate.
      6. Melt the oils. Melt the hard oils and butters gently, then stir in the liquid oils, which also helps cool the mix.
      7. Match temperatures. Let both cool to , within 10°F (5°C) of each other.
      8. Combine. Pour the lye water into the oils, pouring along the stick blender shaft to avoid bubbles. Alternate short pulses with stirring.
      9. Blend to trace. Stop at light trace, when drizzled batter leaves a faint line on the surface, like thin pudding.
      10. Stir in extras. At light trace, stir in by hand, then pour soon: extras can thicken the batter.
      11. Add fragrance and colour. Stir in by hand, plus any colourants.
      12. Pour. Pour into the mold, tap it on the counter to release air bubbles, and texture the top if you like.
      13. Insulate or chill. Decide on gel or no gel (see above) and leave it undisturbed for 24–48 hours.
      14. Unmold and cut. Unmold when it's firm (sodium lactate speeds this up) and cut into bars.
      15. Cure 4–6 weeks. Stand the bars on a rack with air around them, out of direct sun, turning them now and then.
      16. Clean up. Wipe tools with paper towels, or let the batter sit for a day to turn into soap, then wash. If lye gets on your skin, rinse with plenty of cool water.

      Ingredient list for labels

      Do I have to list ingredients?

      In the US, true soap (mostly oils plus lye, sold only to clean) is regulated by the CPSC and doesn't legally need an ingredient list.

      If the label or listing makes cosmetic claims, such as moisturizing, nourishing, deodorizing or scented for aromatherapy, it's regulated by the FDA as a cosmetic. It then needs an ingredient list in descending order of weight. Ingredients at 1% or less can go in any order after the rest. It also needs the net weight, and a US address, phone number or website where customers can report reactions.

      Many soapmakers list ingredients anyway, because customers expect it. Check INCI names against your suppliers' spec sheets. This is general information, not legal advice.
